1. Your first electric vehicle

30 min suggested: notes 8, exploration 10, reflection 2, quiz 10.

  • Distinguish battery-electric, hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles.
  • Identify the battery, motor and charging connection.
  • Explain one benefit, one limitation and one safety boundary.

01 / What makes an EV different?

A battery-electric vehicle (BEV) moves using electric motors supplied by a rechargeable traction battery. It has no petrol or diesel engine for propulsion. A conventional hybrid (HEV) combines an engine and electric drive but is not plugged in for charging. A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) has both propulsion sources and can also recharge from an external supply. “Electric” does not mean every vehicle has the same design. Ask which type is being discussed.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Electric Vehicles

02 / Learn the main parts

The traction battery stores energy; the motor turns a shaft; the transmission carries that motion to the wheels. The charge port connects to charging equipment. The inverter controls the motor’s electrical supply. The smaller low-voltage battery supports accessories and controls, so it has a different job from the traction battery. Use these names to describe the model rather than calling every electrical box a battery.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: How Do All-Electric Cars Work?

03 / Energy is not created in the car

Draw a chain on paper: stored energy, controlled electrical supply, motor, wheels. When a moving vehicle slows using regenerative braking, some energy can return to the battery. Losses mean that not all energy is recovered. The term “self-charging” must not be interpreted as unlimited driving without an energy source. A stationary parked BEV does not refill itself merely because its wheels are connected to a motor.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: How Do All-Electric Cars Work?

04 / Think in journeys, not slogans

For this course, use a fictional 40 km return commute. List where the vehicle could park, when charging would fit into the day and what a backup plan might be. Do not assume that every home has a suitable socket, that every workplace provides charging, or that all public chargers are available. This is a planning exercise, not a recommendation to buy a particular vehicle.

05 / Two statements can both be true

A BEV produces no exhaust at its own tailpipe during driving. Manufacturing the vehicle and producing the electricity can still have environmental impacts. “No tailpipe exhaust” is therefore more precise than “no impact anywhere.” Electric driving can offer benefits, but suitability depends on a person’s journeys, charging access and budget. In this course you will learn to explain conditions, not repeat a sales claim.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Electric Vehicle Benefits and Considerations

06 / Your first safety rule

Inspect and disassemble only the virtual vehicle here. On a real EV, a switched-off dashboard does not prove that high-voltage parts are safe to touch. This awareness course does not train or authorise you to open a pack, disconnect high-voltage cables or perform electrical testing. Keep physical servicing separate from on-screen exploration.

Source: UK Health and Safety Executive: Electric and hybrid vehicles (hazard principles, not South African law)

Explore: Find the three essentials

  1. Open the complete vehicle and use Body off to see beneath the shell.
  2. Identify the traction battery, motor and driven wheels using their labels.
  3. Trace the path from the battery to the motor. Return here and explain the path without looking.
Text alternative: Without 3D, use this sequence: battery → inverter → motor → transmission → wheels. The charge port is a separate connection used to replenish the battery.

Reflection

In two or three sentences, explain the difference between a BEV and a PHEV to someone in your community. Name one question they should ask about charging.

2. Batteries, energy and range

40 min suggested: notes 10, exploration 12, reflection 6, quiz 12.

  • Distinguish a cell, module and pack.
  • Read kW, kWh, state of charge and state of health correctly.
  • Use a simple energy budget while recognising its assumptions.

01 / Start small: cell, module, pack

A cell is one electrochemical building block. In this atlas, eight cells form a module and twelve modules form the illustrated pack. Other vehicle designs can arrange cells differently or omit traditional modules. The model is a reference example, not the universal construction of every EV. Inspecting it virtually helps you name the parts without making a claim about a real manufacturer.

02 / What happens inside a cell?

A rechargeable cell stores energy through changes in chemical state. Electrons move through the external circuit while ions move through electrolyte inside the cell. A separator physically separates electrodes while allowing ionic transport. The animation is deliberately enlarged: it is not a video of atoms or a reason to open a physical battery. Repeated charging and use can change materials over time.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy: DOE Explains Batteries

03 / Power versus energy

A kilowatt (kW) is a rate of energy transfer. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is an amount of energy. A 10 kW load maintained for 2 hours uses 20 kWh in an ideal calculation. Do not compare a motor’s kW rating directly with a battery’s kWh capacity as if the units meant the same thing. Work through this example using a kettle or another familiar load as an analogy.

04 / Reading voltage and charge capacity

For the reference values, nominal energy in kWh is voltage multiplied by ampere-hours, divided by 1000. At 300 V and 200 Ah, nominal energy is 60 kWh. In a series string of identical cells, voltages add but ampere-hour capacity does not. Parallel strings can add ampere-hour capacity. This is arithmetic for learning, not an instruction to assemble batteries. Usable energy may be below the nominal value.

05 / Charge, health and management

State of charge (SoC) estimates how full a battery is at a particular time. State of health (SoH) describes condition relative to a reference, often including capacity or resistance. A full battery is not necessarily a new battery. A battery management system monitors conditions such as cell voltage and temperature and can limit operation. Cooling helps manage temperature; none of these features makes physical battery work safe for an untrained person.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Maintenance and Safety of Electric Vehicles

06 / A range estimate is a budget

In a fictional example, 48 kWh available for a journey at 0.16 kWh/km gives 48 ÷ 0.16 = 300 km before any further reserve. If you deliberately reserve 8 kWh of that 48 kWh, the planning calculation becomes 40 ÷ 0.16 = 250 km. Real consumption varies with route, speed, temperature, payload and auxiliary loads. Do not present an estimate as a guaranteed arrival distance.

Explore: Follow the battery hierarchy

  1. Open the pack, select a module and use Open module.
  2. Select a cell and use Open cell. Read the layer labels.
  3. Explain why a full-pack voltage and a single-cell voltage are different quantities.
Text alternative: Reference hierarchy: 12 modules × 8 cells = 96 cells. For 3.2 V nominal cells all in series: 96 × 3.2 = 307.2 V nominal. At 200 Ah this is 61.44 kWh nominal, not guaranteed usable energy.

Reflection

Your fictional journey needs 32 kWh and you plan an 8 kWh reserve. Explain why 40 kWh available would be a planning threshold, not a guarantee. Name two factors that could change consumption.

3. From electricity to moving wheels

50 min suggested: notes 12, exploration 18, reflection 8, quiz 12.

  • Explain stator, rotor, inverter and DC/DC functions.
  • Follow the sequence from POWER to READY to driving.
  • Describe regeneration without implying energy creation.

01 / Follow one path

Use the chain battery → inverter → motor → reduction drive → wheels. Each arrow represents a transfer, not a new source of energy. The inverter controls the motor’s electrical supply; the motor produces mechanical torque. A gearbox can reduce rotational speed while increasing torque at its output, with losses. The mechanical connection does not make the battery charge continuously while driving.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: How Do All-Electric Cars Work?

02 / Stator and rotor

The stator is the stationary part of the machine. Its windings carry controlled currents. The rotor turns inside it and is attached to a shaft. In the permanent-magnet reference motor, magnetic interaction produces torque. Phased currents create a rotating magnetic pattern, not a physically rotating stator. The alternative induction rotor illustrates a different construction. The atlas field arrows teach direction and motion; they are not measured magnetic-flux data.

Source: MathWorks: Surface Mount PMSM, construction and operating equations

03 / Why POWER is not “drive”

The reference simulator first wakes controls, performs checks and pre-charges the DC link before reaching READY. Pre-charge limits the initial charging current into the inverter’s capacitors. READY means the reference system permits driving; it is not an accelerator command. In the virtual lab, select D and apply the accelerator to request motion. Real start-up sequences and driver interfaces differ. Never copy an on-screen disassembly or powered cutaway on a real vehicle.

04 / Two converters, two jobs

The traction inverter manages the motor supply. The DC/DC converter supplies a lower-voltage electrical system from the traction battery. Do not confuse either with the on-board charger used during AC charging. Controls, lights and communication can depend on the low-voltage system even when the traction battery still contains energy. A vehicle that will not become READY therefore does not automatically have an empty traction battery.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: How Do All-Electric Cars Work?

05 / Slow down and follow the return path

During regenerative braking the drive system can act as a generator, returning some energy toward the battery. Friction brakes remain necessary. Conditions such as battery charge level, temperature and traction can limit regeneration; follow the actual vehicle’s driving instructions. Reducing speed in the virtual model lets you compare motoring and braking without implying that the same energy is recovered in both cases.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Maintenance and Safety of Electric Vehicles

06 / Observe rather than assume

Use the atlas to test three predictions: the rotor turns while the stator remains fixed; READY alone leaves wheel speed at zero; a lower-voltage supply is a different quantity from the traction DC bus. Write the prediction first, run the virtual activity and record whether the model supports it. Numerical readings use model time, while visible rotation is slowed for teaching. Neither is live vehicle telemetry.

Explore: Watch a magnetic field produce motion

  1. Open the motor activity. It starts the reference driving demo and exposes the magnetic-field illustration.
  2. Use Pause, then Step. Identify the fixed stator and moving rotor; turn on phase-current instruments if useful.
  3. Return to Complete vehicle to relate shaft and wheel movement. These are virtual observations only.
Text alternative: Fixed stator windings create a rotating magnetic pattern. Magnetic interaction produces rotor torque. The rotor shaft transfers motion through a reduction drive to the wheels. The inverter controls the electrical excitation.

Reflection

Explain to a new learner why switching POWER on is not the same as pressing an accelerator. Then explain what remains stationary inside a running motor.

4. Charging in South Africa

60 min suggested: notes 15, exploration 18, reflection 15, quiz 12.

  • Distinguish AC charging from DC charging.
  • Estimate charging time and an illustrative energy cost.
  • Build a practical, location-specific charging checklist.

01 / Where conversion happens

During AC charging, the on-board charger converts incoming AC to the DC needed by the battery. During DC charging, conversion is performed outside the vehicle and DC is delivered through a compatible vehicle charging path. The BMS and charging controls still enforce limits. A plug shape or a large advertised power number does not guarantee compatibility or actual charging speed.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: How Do All-Electric Cars Work?

02 / Compatibility is a checklist

Before planning a stop, check the vehicle inlet, connector, supported AC or DC mode, vehicle charging limit and the operator’s instructions. Names such as Type 2 and CCS2 identify connector systems; do not assume that every vehicle accepts every connector or adaptor. Look up the exact model and station specification. Check payment access and whether you need your own cable. This course supplies a checklist, not a live compatibility guarantee.

03 / Power and time

A useful ideal estimate is time = energy to add ÷ average power actually entering the battery. Adding 24 kWh at a constant net 6 kW takes 4 hours. A charger’s peak rating is not necessarily the average delivered power. Vehicle limits, charging losses, battery temperature and reduced power near high SoC can lengthen the session. A constant-power classroom calculation is a starting point, not a booking promise.

04 / A rand-based example, not a tariff

Suppose a session stores 20 kWh in the battery with an assumed 90% wall-to-battery efficiency. It draws 20 ÷ 0.90 = 22.22 kWh from the supply. At a fictional energy-only price of R3.00/kWh, the example cost is R66.67. Real tariffs and operator fees vary: check your own bill or the session price, including any parking, time or connection fees. Never present R3.00 as a national current tariff.

05 / Make a local plan

Choose a destination that matters to you, then inspect an operator’s current map. GridCars provides a South African charging map and session information; use it as one source rather than a promise that a port is free or working. Confirm location, opening hours, access restrictions, connector, displayed price, status and a backup. For a supply interruption, preserve your travel reserve and check alternatives rather than assuming the next charger is available.

Source: GridCars: operator information and live-map access for South Africa

06 / Home and shared-property charging

A charging installation needs a competent assessment of the equipment and electrical supply. Do not turn a classroom power calculation into wiring instructions. Consult the property owner where necessary and qualified installers for suitability and required approvals. Do not use damaged equipment or improvise adaptors and extension arrangements. Solar generation or backup power does not automatically establish EV-charging compatibility; the whole installation must be appropriately designed.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Maintenance and Safety of Electric Vehicles

Explore: Locate the charging path

  1. Open the on-board charger and identify its input, conversion stages and output.
  2. In the atlas Electrical view compare the AC charging path with the DC charging path.
  3. Return here and explain why charging-station power and actual battery charging power need not be equal.
Text alternative: AC path: supply → charging equipment → on-board charger → battery. DC path: external conversion equipment → compatible DC charging path → battery. Controls and battery limits still apply.

Reflection

Plan a fictional 200 km trip. Record how you would check a suitable charging stop, a backup, access, payment and a reserve. Do not use this worksheet as confirmation of current charger availability.

5. Safety and informed EV choices

70 min suggested: notes 15, exploration 20, reflection 20, quiz 15.

  • Recognise when to stop and call trained help.
  • Distinguish tailpipe impacts from life-cycle impacts.
  • Build an evidence-based ownership and awareness plan.

01 / Awareness is not authorisation

Use this course to recognise hazards, ask better questions and know when specialist help is necessary. It is not a qualification in high-voltage repair, charger installation, emergency response or vehicle conversion. A virtual exploded view deliberately separates powered parts in a way that would be unsafe or impossible on a real vehicle. Never use it as a physical procedure.

02 / Recognise a stop situation

Damaged high-voltage cabling, a damaged pack, smoke, unusual heat or other serious warning signs are not invitations to investigate by touch. Keep yourself and others away, seek trained assistance and follow emergency instructions. Do not cut cables, open an enclosure or attempt to remove a battery. Stored energy can remain after switch-off. For an emergency in South Africa, 112 is a mobile-phone emergency number; give your location and describe the incident.

Source: UK Health and Safety Executive: Electric and hybrid vehicles (hazard principles, not South African law)

Source: Western Cape Government: Know who you can call in an emergency

03 / Maintenance still matters

A BEV does not need every item associated with a combustion engine, but it is not maintenance-free. Tyres, brakes, cooling and other vehicle systems still need the manufacturer’s specified care. Battery servicing and recovery should follow manufacturer guidance through competent personnel. Do not discard traction cells in household waste or turn an old pack into an unassessed home-storage project; ask the manufacturer or an appropriate battery handler about return and end-of-life arrangements.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Maintenance and Safety of Electric Vehicles

04 / Compare the whole life cycle

Tailpipe exhaust is only one boundary. A broader comparison includes vehicle and battery manufacture, electricity or fuel production, operation and end-of-life handling. The result depends on the vehicle, distance travelled, energy use, electricity source and study assumptions. For a South African discussion, do not copy a percentage from another country without checking its boundary and electricity assumptions. This course makes no universal emissions-saving claim.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, AFDC: Electric Vehicle Benefits and Considerations

05 / Build a decision sheet

For a fictional household or community fleet, list daily and longest journeys, parking, charging access, reserve and backup, purchase and finance costs, insurance, servicing, tyres, energy and potential installation costs. Check battery-warranty terms and exclusions for the actual vehicle. Repeat the budget with a different electricity price and consumption assumption. A result that changes when assumptions change should be presented as conditional, not certain.

06 / Teach back, accurately

Prepare a two-minute explanation for a friend: what the vehicle does, where charging would happen, a simple energy example and the point at which trained help is required. Correct the claim “an EV has no environmental impacts anywhere” without replacing it with another absolute claim. Share the course, not another learner’s scores or personal information. Further study can lead into electrical machines, power electronics or accredited occupational training, but this certificate itself does not confer those credentials.

Explore: Identify protective and control functions

  1. Inspect the control assembly labels and distinguish monitoring from propulsion.
  2. Revisit the battery view to identify the BMS, contactors and protective components virtually.
  3. Use your notes-only alternative if 3D is unavailable: explain why a warning must be escalated instead of bypassed.
Text alternative: The BMS monitors battery conditions; controllers manage permissions; contactors switch a power path; protection and sensing are not substitutes for trained people or manufacturer procedures.

Reflection

Create a five-point EV-awareness message for a South African audience: vehicle type, energy use, charging plan, safety boundary and one claim that needs verification. This reflection is for learning and is not automatically graded.
